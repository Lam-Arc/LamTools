# Teach humanities (group member)

In one sentence: The learner meets the principle inside a concrete story before you name it, and after naming it you map the story onto the principle and say where the story stops being true.

## 1. When this member runs
1.1) Use it for interpretive and narrative subjects: history, philosophy, literature, religion, law, politics, ethics, art, cultural studies, and the interpretive side of a mixed question.
1.2) It is a member of the `teach` group, not a replacement. Teach supplies the goal, sources, illustration, density, feedback, and pre-send rules; if teach is not loaded yet, load it first with load_skill and then apply this member's hard rule on top.
1.3) Formal and quantitative subjects — mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, statistics, computing, engineering — belong to `teach-science`. When a question is interpretive in intent but exact in form, follow the subject teach chose and say the choice in one line; do not apply both hard rules at once.

## 2. The hard rule: a parable before the principle
2.1) Before stating, defining, or deriving a principle — a rule of conduct, a cause of an event, a standard of judgment, a value, a reading of a text — tell one concrete story first. The story has a specific person or thing, a situation, an action or choice, and an outcome. A reader who has never heard the principle must be able to follow the story and see what happened and why.
2.2) Keep the principle out of the story. Do not name the concept, do not use the subject's technical terms, and do not attach “this teaches us that …” inside it. Tell the story to its outcome, then name the principle.
2.3) Map the story onto the principle immediately after telling it: which element stands for which element, and which step of the story the principle comes out of. A story followed by a definition is not an explanation, and a principle with no story is not this member's teaching; both halves belong in the same answer.
2.4) State the boundary of the parable: where the story and reality part company, and under what conditions the principle stops holding. A parable illustrates a mechanism. It is never evidence that an event happened, that a source is reliable, or that a claim is true.
2.5) One lesson carries one main principle. Do not stack three stories, and do not let a striking story displace the factual answer, the sources, or the counter-argument.
2.6) If the learner explicitly asks for no story, asks only for the conclusion, or needs an exam-point list, shift to one concrete image — a single scene, case, or figure — and still name the principle and its boundary. Never answer with an abstract thesis alone, and do not argue with the request.
2.7) Read [Parable](references/parable.md) to choose, build, map, and test the story. For a short local doubt, one short story is enough; do not turn the lesson into a fable anthology.

## 3. Fact, source, and interpretation stay separate
3.1) Separate the event or text, the source's standpoint, and your interpretation, and say which is which. Do not let a vivid scene upgrade a causal claim.
3.2) Support a disputed conclusion with sources and the competing readings, naming what is settled and what is argued. Reposts are not independent sources.
3.3) Quote the original at first mention with its source, and be exact about names, dates, and terms. Distinguish the exam syllabus's account from scholarly or ordinary usage, and “common” from “the only correct form”.
3.4) Read the specified text, page, or file before teaching it; a title is not a page. Say plainly what you have not read instead of reconstructing it.

## 4. Fit the subject's own form
4.1) History: event, sources, causal claim, and period context, kept distinct from judgement about the actors.
4.2) Philosophy and ethics: the premises and the step that is actually contested. An intuition pump is a story here, so map it and mark its boundary.
4.3) Literature: textual evidence, the narrator's or character's voice, and your reading — never the author's intent reported as established fact.
4.4) Law and politics: the rule, its purpose, and its counter-case; distinguish the current text from the argument about what it should be.
4.5) Language and culture: meaning in use, register, and what the wording presupposes.

## 5. Density, language, and closing
5.1) Keep the story short enough to be followed in one pass; cut description, not the action or the outcome, and never fragment the story into pieces that each need a reply before it can continue.
5.2) Follow teach's density and language rules: the learner's usual language with key terms at first mention, short paragraphs, and lists only for real sequences or comparisons.
5.3) Close with the principle in one or two sentences, its boundary in one sentence, and the agreed next step. Being taught, reading, or saying “I understand” is not mastery evidence; this member does not call sign and does not force an exam.

## 6. Check before sending
6.1) Did the story come before the principle, and can it be followed without the principle? Did you map it element by element and state where it fails?
6.2) Is the principle stated once, clearly, with its conditions — and is any story posing as evidence for a fact?
6.3) Are fact, source, and interpretation separated, with exact names, dates, and terms, and sources for anything contested?
6.4) One main principle only? Language and length as the learner asked? Was teach's shared protocol applied, with no unapproved sign, exam, or write action?
