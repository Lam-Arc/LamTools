# Parables: build the story, map it, bound it

Read when you need a story before a principle, when the learner says the example did not help, or when a story risks becoming the evidence.

## 1. What the story must do

1.1) Carry the mechanism, not the moral. The story shows how something comes about; the principle names it afterwards.
1.2) Be complete in four beats: someone (a specific person or thing), a situation, a choice or event, an outcome. If a beat is missing, the reader has to guess and the mapping will not hold.
1.3) Be followable with no subject vocabulary. If the story cannot be understood without the term you are about to teach, it is not a parable yet.
1.4) Be short. One to three short paragraphs is usually enough; a story the learner skims has not been told.

## 2. Map it, then bound it

2.1) Name the story elements and what each stands for, one by one. The mapping is where the teaching happens; a story the learner enjoyed but cannot map has taught nothing.
2.2) Say which step of the story the principle comes out of, so the principle reads as a conclusion about the story rather than an assertion placed next to it.
2.3) State the boundary in the same answer: where the story and reality differ, which factor the story leaves out, and when the principle no longer applies.
2.4) Never let the story serve as evidence. A vivid scene is not a source, a measurement, or proof that an event happened.

## 3. Worked example: survivorship bias

Story: planes come back from a bombing run with bullet holes all over their wings, and the engineers set out to armour the wings. One of them asks where the holes are not — the engines. The wings are full of holes because those planes made it home; the planes hit in the engines did not. The armour goes on the engines.

Principle, named after the story: the sample you can observe is not the population. When the unobserved cases were removed by the very process you are studying, the visible pattern can point the wrong way.

Mapping: returning planes = the observed sample; wing holes = visible evidence; empty engines = the missing cases; the conclusion is read from an absence, not from a presence.

Boundary: the story assumes the record only ever reaches survivors and that added weight is worth the trade. It does not apply where the sample is drawn at random, and it does not by itself say which parts are critical.

What it must not be used for: asserting a particular historical decision, or replacing real data with an anecdote.

## 4. Worked example: a procedural rule (presumption of innocence)

Story: a village accuses a stranger of stealing grain. The elders decide by who tells the better story; the stranger speaks the dialect badly, loses, and is banished. A year later the real thief confesses. The rule that follows: the accuser must establish the case, and doubt is resolved in the accused's favour.

Mapping: the elders' decision = the tribunal; the better story = unverified evidence; the banishment = an error that cannot be undone.

Boundary: the story shows why the burden of proof matters when evidence is weak. It does not mean every accusation is false, and it does not fix the standard of proof, which differs by proceeding.

## 5. Failure modes to check

- The conclusion stated inside the story, so the learner gets the moral without the experience.
- A story told and then abandoned: no mapping, or a definition dropped in its place.
- Several stories stacked on one principle, blurring which one carried it.
- Story elements that do not correspond to the principle's elements, leaving the reader to force the analogy.
- A story presented as proof, evidence, or a source for a factual claim.
- A story that misstates a mechanism to be more moving, replacing a real cause with a tidier one.
- The story kept after the learner asked for the conclusion only, or after an advanced learner asked for the argument — shift to one concrete image and keep the boundary.
- The story filling the whole answer at the cost of the subject's terms, dates, or the counter-argument.

## 6. Check before sending

6.1) Could a reader who has never heard the principle retell the story and say what happened?
6.2) Is every element of the story matched to an element of the principle, and is the boundary stated?
6.3) Is the story doing illustration only, with facts, sources, and interpretation kept separate?
